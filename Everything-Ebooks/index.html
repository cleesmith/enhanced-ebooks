    1. Five Doors Same Room
    FIVE DOORS SAME ROOM
    (And One Basement Staircase We Don't Talk About)
    Alex stared at the manuscript file—400 pages of anxiety, coffee, and questionable 3:47 AM decisions saved as NovelDraft_FINAL_FINAL_v2.docx.
    Every writer knows this moment. The manuscript is done (lie). It just needs a quick edit (a bigger lie). And surely this won't take long (the biggest lie of all).
    "Alright," Alex muttered, opening EverythingEbooks. "Let's see if you can edit without destroying my voice."
    The interface was calmer than expected. No intimidating buttons screaming UPLOAD NOW. No immediate demand for credit cards. No JOIN NOW, PAY LATER. Just a simple question: How would you like to get your manuscript into EverythingEbooks?
    Five doors appeared—five perfectly reasonable ways to start.
    Well. Four perfectly reasonable ways, and one basement staircase that nobody mentions at writers' conferences.
    DOOR ONE: Open > New
    (The "Fresh Start, Clean Slate" Route)
    Some writers arrive with nothing but an idea and the audacity to believe they can turn it into 80,000 words. This door is for them.
    Jordan, a human editor who'd seen it all, appreciated this door's honesty. "There's something brave about starting with nothing," Jordan would say, adjusting glasses that perpetually slipped when reading difficult passages. "No baggage. No formatting disasters from 2019. Just possibility."
    Click New and you're in. Create your sections and chapters. Start typing. The built-in editor isn't fancy—it's not trying to be Word or Docs. It's a clean space to write that creates your ebook directly. No switching between apps. No upload process. You're already where you need to be.
    This appeals to a specific kind of writer. The one who's tired of feature creep, who doesn't need another button, another option, another distraction. Just a place to put words in order.
    Alex tried this door for several sections of this book. Typed directly in EverythingEbooks. There's something meditative about the simplicity.
    Just you and the words.
    DOOR TWO: Open > Load EPUB
    (The "I Found This On My Hard Drive" Path)
    Somewhere on your computer, there's a folder. Maybe it's called "Old Projects" or "Writing Stuff" or "DO NOT DELETE" in all caps because past-you was dramatic. Inside that folder, buried under three subfolders and an alarming number of cat photos, lives an EPUB file.
    Maybe it's a novel you published years ago that needs updating. Maybe it's a project you abandoned in 2021 that's been whispering your name at 2 AM. Maybe you found it on an old SD card and thought, "Wait, I wrote this?"
    This door is for all of them.
    Load EPUB pulls your existing ebook from wherever it's been hiding—your hard drive, Google Drive, that SD card you forgot you owned. The file arrives intact, chapters preserved, ready for whatever work it needs.
    Maya used this door when she decided her debut novel needed a second edition. Three years of reader feedback, a few embarrassing typos that haunted her dreams, and one character name she'd somehow spelled two different ways for 300 pages. She uploaded PuertoRicanGothic.epub and watched it load, every chapter exactly where she'd left it.
    "It's like opening a time capsule," she said. "Except the time capsule is full of adverbs I should have cut the first time."
    DOOR THREE: Load from Library
    (The "I Live Here Now" Option)
    Here's a secret about writers who use EverythingEbooks: some of them never leave.
    The app has a library—a place where your ebooks live, where you can read them, where they wait. And when you're ready to edit again, you don't need to dig through folders or remember which cloud service you uploaded to. Your book is right there.
    This door is for the iterators. The writers who bounce between writing and reading, who need to experience their work as a reader before returning as an author. Write a chapter. Export to library. Read it on your phone during lunch. Notice that Chapter 7 drags in the middle. Load from library. Fix it. Repeat.
    Jordan calls this "the professional door." Not because the other doors are amateur, but because this workflow suggests you've been here before. You know the rhythm. Write, read, revise. The library becomes part of your process, not just storage.
    One writer in Rae Kim's online community forum described it perfectly: "I used to email manuscripts to myself just to read them fresh. Now I just load from library. It's the same ritual, minus the inbox chaos."
    DOOR FOUR: Load DOCX -> EPUB
    (The "My Life Is In Microsoft Word" Reality)
    Most of us live behind this door. Let's be honest about it.
    Years of manuscripts in Microsoft Word, cluttered with tracked changes from that one friend who thinks they're an editor. Comments in the margins questioning your life choices. Formatting that somehow got bizarre around Chapter 12 and you never figured out why.

When she finally used Load DOCX -> EPUB with PuertoRicanGothic_FINAL.docx, she held her breath. Last time she'd trusted a tool with her manuscript, it had "corrected" every bit of Spanish dialogue and turned her protagonist's voice into what she called "beige suburban nothing."

    The Google Docs variation works the same way. Download your Doc as a .docx file (File > Download > Microsoft Word), then load it through this door. Same door, different handle.

Loaded into EverythingEbooks. The conversion took seconds, which left plenty of time for anxiety about what would happen next.

Your manuscript arrives intact, ready for transformation, with all your carefully chosen fonts stripped away. Let's be honest: manuscripts don't need seventeen fonts. Just words.
    DOOR FIVE: AI Writing - the Basement Stairs
    (The "I Have Nothing But I Need Something" Descent)
    There's a fifth door. It's not at eye level with the others. You have to look down to see it, like a basement entrance that's been there all along but nobody mentions in polite company.

    I'm a writer, I want me some words.
    But down here, pride gets left at the top of the stairs. This is where you admit defeat to the blank page and hiss, "Fine. You write it. I'll fix it later. I swear."
    The descent has stages. First, you Chat—brainstorming with AI, throwing ideas at the wall, watching some stick and others slide. Then you take those conversations and build a Brainstorm, something that might become structure. From there, an Outline emerges, chapters taking shape like constellations from scattered stars. World Building follows—characters and places materializing from the collaborative dream state. And finally, you write, one chapter at a time, AI and author passing the work back and forth.
    Maya tried this descent once, during what she calls The Dark November. Writer's block had won. She'd been staring at a blank page for weeks. Finally, she took the stairs.
    "Write me a chapter about a woman returning home after twenty years," she typed into the chat, feeling like she was cheating on her muse.
    The AI gave her 3,000 words of purple prose that would make a romance novelist blush. Her protagonist's eyes were "cerulean orbs reflecting the weight of unspoken truths." The house was "a monument to memories that whispered secrets in the language of creaking floorboards."
    Maya laughed. Then she cried. Then she started editing.
    And here's the thing nobody tells you about the basement: Sometimes bad words are better than no words. Sometimes you need that pile of purple prose just to have something to fix. You can edit garbage; you can't edit a blank page. Every "cerulean orb" you change to "blue eyes" is progress. Every "whispered secret" you delete is a decision made.
    The AI Writing tools know what they are. They're not trying to be Hemingway or Morrison or even that person who writes those cozy mysteries your mom loves. They're offering you clay. Messy, purple, overwrought clay that you can shape into something real.
    The stages are all there in the basement, each one covered in cobwebs from writers who don't admit they visit:
    Chat (where ideas first surface, messy and undefined) Brainstorm (where chaos starts finding shape) Outline (where chapters emerge from the fog) World (where characters and places take form) Chapter Writing (where rough drafts happen, one piece at a time)
    Jordan calls it "The Necessary Embarrassment." That moment when you let AI write something terrible just to break the seal on the blank page. "I suspect Pulitzer winners start with AI-generated garbage," Jordan says. "They just don't tell anyone about the basement."

    Alex stared at them. “You're telling me editing my entire manuscript comes down to picking from a menu?”
    The dropdown didn't answer. Dropdowns rarely do.
    Here's the thing nobody tells you about editing: It's not one thing. It's many things wearing a trench coat pretending to be one thing.
    There's the big-picture stuff—does your plot make sense, do your characters grow, does Chapter 7 contradict Chapter 23?
    There's the sentence-level stuff—is this clear, is this tight, did you use “very” seventeen times on one page?
    There's the nitpicky stuff—is it “lay” or “lie,” do you capitalize Dad when it's used as a name, should you have a comma there or are you just comma-happy at 3:47 AM?
    EverythingEbooks doesn't pretend these are the same job. Instead, it gives you a toolbox. You pick the tool you need. You send it. You see what it finds. You decide what to do about it.
    After the tool finished running, Alex clicked “Report” which instantly became an ebook. Wow, everything really is ebooks.
    He clicked on "Library" to go read it. He used Chrome's split view to read the ebook report while still editing his ebook.
    “Chapter 3, Scene 2: The main character finds the key before she knows it exists. Problem: contradicts discovery scene in Chapter 5. Suggestion: move the key discovery or add a hint.”
    Alex scrolled down. More observations appeared like helpful ghosts pointing at the bodies buried in his backyard:
    “Weather continuity issue: Chapter 7 opens with a blizzard. Chapter 8, same day, characters are having a picnic. No explanation for weather change.”
    “Point of view error: Chapter 12, paragraph 3—Sarah knows about the letter, but she wasn't present when it was read and no one told her about it.”
    “Plot logic note: The subplot about the missing painting is introduced in Chapter 4 but never resolved or mentioned after Chapter 9.”
    Alex sat back. “Oh. Oh no.”
    He'd written this manuscript over six months. He knew every character, every scene, every plot thread. Or he thought he did. Apparently, he'd also created a blizzard that vanished between chapters, a psychic character who knew things she couldn't possibly know, and a painting that disappeared into the Bermuda Triangle of abandoned subplots.
    The editing process waited. No judgment. No mockery. Just a quiet: Well? What are you going to do about it?
    Alex started typing fixes. Moved the key discovery to Chapter 5. Added a line about the blizzard clearing by morning. Rewrote Sarah's section from a different character's perspective. Made a note about the painting—either resolve it or cut those early references.
    After each change, the app updated itself. The tools didn't argue. Didn't insist. Just offered observations and let him decide.
    “This is editing,” Alex realized. “This is actual editing. Not someone rewriting my book. Just someone pointing at the holes while I decide how to patch them.”
    MAYA'S UNRESOLVED THREADS
    Many time zones away, Maya sat in her own version of The Night Kitchen—a spare bedroom at 1 AM, laptop balanced on her knees, her bilingual manuscript glowing like a beacon of unfinished business.
    She'd survived the upload (Chapter 1). She'd conquered the API key anxiety (Chapter 2). Now came the part she'd been avoiding: facing all the things she knew were wrong but had hoped no one would notice.
    Maya selected Core Editing Tools, then Narrative Unresolved Analysis. The name alone made her nervous. She followed the same path as Alex.
    “Chapter 2: Mysterious locket introduced. Never mentioned again after this chapter.”
    Maya groaned. She'd completely forgotten about that locket. It was supposed to be important. It had belonged to the protagonist's grandmother. It was supposed to mean something. Instead, it got introduced and then wandered off into the narrative wilderness, never to be seen again.
    “Chapter 9: Cooking contest setup and foreshadowing. No contest scene follows. No resolution provided.”
    “Oh, come on,” Maya muttered. But the tool was right. She'd spent three paragraphs setting up this contest, making it seem crucial to the plot, and then... nothing. The contest never happened. Her protagonist's carefully developed cooking skills never got their moment.
    “Chapter 5: Foreshadowing about trip home to Puerto Rico. Trip never occurs in manuscript.”
    Maya closed her eyes. “I was going to write that scene. I was definitely going to write that scene.”
    But she hadn't. And now her manuscript promised readers a homecoming that never came. A Chekhov's gun that never fired. A narrative thread that just sort of... frayed.
    The tool wasn't judging her. It was just pointing out what it had found, like a friend saying, “Hey, you left your keys in the door.”
    Maya started editing. Added a scene where the locket opened, revealing a photo that explained its significance. Cut the cooking contest setup entirely—if she wasn't going to write the scene, she couldn't promise it to readers. And the trip home? She kept that foreshadowing but added it to her sequel notes. Intentionally unresolved for Book Two.
    One thread she left hanging on purpose. One question unanswered. Because some stories don't wrap up every loose end, and that was her choice to make.
    The tools offered suggestions. Maya made decisions. That was the partnership.
    JORDAN'S LINE EDITING
    Jordan approached EverythingEbooks the way a mechanic approaches an engine: methodically, professionally, with reading glasses that wouldn't stay put and a tolerance for tedium that bordered on supernatural.
    He selected "Core Editing Tools", then "Line Editing by Chapter". Clicked "Send". But line editing had a trick the other tools didn't.
    When the tool finished, Jordan saw two buttons: Report and One-by-one. He'd used Report before—an ebook of the AI's report. Useful for scanning everything at once. But today he clicked "One-by-one".
    The interface shifted. Instead of a report to wade through, he got a single issue. Issue 1 of 25.
    A panel appeared below the editor with the problem sentence highlighted in the text: "Spud was born in the cellar of the old farmhouse during the most coldest February anyone could remember."
    In the panel, four sections appeared:
    ORIGINAL: "Spud was born in the cellar of the old farmhouse during the most coldest February anyone could remember."
    ISSUES: "1. Incorrect degree: 'most coldest' doubles the superlative. 2. Clarity/flow: 'during' + 'anyone could remember' works, but tightening increases punch."
    SUGGESTED: "Spud was born in the cellar of the old farmhouse during the coldest February anyone could remember."
    EXPLANATION: "This keeps your tone and meaning while correcting the grammar and preserving the stark, wintry setup."
    Buttons waited: Accept, Custom, Skip, Close.
    Jordan pushed up his glasses. "Now this is how I want to edit."
    He clicked Accept. The sentence updated.
    He clicked Next. Issue 2 of 25 appeared.
    The next one was trickier—a suggestion to tighten a sentence that Jordan thought needed its length for rhythm. He clicked Skip. The tool moved on without argument.
    Issue 7 was close but not quite right. Jordan clicked Custom, typed his own revision, and continued.
    This was the difference between reading a report and having a conversation. Each suggestion got its moment. Each decision was deliberate. No scanning, no missing things, no "I'll come back to that one later" and then forgetting.
    But the real trick? It kept him awake. Every issue demanded a click. Accept. Custom. Skip. No choice meant no progress. Jordan had read manuscripts where his eyes glazed over by page fifty, where he'd realize he'd "read" three paragraphs without absorbing a word. One-by-one made that impossible. The app wouldn't let him nod off. It asked a question and waited for an answer.
    The tool flagged sentences that were too long, words that were overused, phrases that could be tighter. But it didn't just correct—it explained why:
    "This sentence is 47 words long. Consider breaking into two sentences for clarity?"
    "'Very' appears six times on this page. Consider stronger base adjectives?"
    "Cliché detected: 'avoid it like the plague.' Consider fresh phrasing?"
    Jordan accepted some suggestions, ignored others. A long sentence wasn't always wrong—sometimes rhythm mattered more than rules. "Very" could stay if the character would actually say it. And sometimes a cliché was exactly what the character would reach for.
    One by one, issue by issue, the manuscript got cleaner. Not because the tool rewrote it, but because Jordan made twenty-five individual decisions about his words. Accept. Custom. Skip. Each choice his.
    The tool made suggestions. Jordan made judgment calls. His glasses slid down his nose. He pushed them back up. The work continued.

    THE CUSTOMIZATION SECRET
    (The Button You Might Have Missed)
    A week later, Maya was in the forum asking about the Copy Editing tool. “It keeps flagging my intentional sentence fragments. I know they're fragments. They're supposed to be fragments. How do I make it stop?”
    Rae replied within minutes: “Click ”Prompt” before you send it.”
    Maya stared at her screen. “The what now?”
    “The Prompt button. In front of Send. You can modify what the tool does.”
    This was the secret hiding in plain sight. That Prompt button Alex had seen but been too tired to click? It was the key to making these tools actually yours.
    Here's what nobody tells you at first: Each tool isn't some locked, unchangeable process. Each tool is just a prompt—a set of instructions combined with your manuscript. And you can edit those instructions.
    Jordan discovered this when he wanted Copy Editing to focus only on grammar, not style suggestions. He clicked “Prompt,” read the tool's default instructions, and added a line: “Focus only on grammar and mechanics. Do not make style suggestions.”
    Clicked "Send" again. The tool obeyed.
    Alex used it when he wanted Narrative Internal Analysis to pay extra attention to a specific subplot. Clicked Prompt. Added: “Pay special attention to the timeline of the heist subplot in Chapters 8-15.”
    The tool did exactly that.
    Maya used it to protect her intentional choices. Clicked Prompt before sending Line Editing. Added: “Do not flag intentional sentence fragments used for dramatic effect or character voice. Do not suggest changes to Spanish dialogue or code-switching.”
    The tool respected her boundaries.
    The workflow became clear:
    1. select your Tool
    2. if you want to customize it, click Prompt and modify the instructions
    3. click Send
    4. wait for the Tool to finish
    5. click Report to see results
    6. make your edits
    7. click "send Ebook"
    8. if you want to run the same tool again with your changes, just hit Clear and Send again
    9. if the results still aren't what you want, click Prompt again and tweak it
    Each tool is just a prompt combined with your manuscript. You can edit the prompt, send, look at results, edit your manuscript, run the same tool again, or try a different one. There's no fixed sequence. No mandatory order. No “you must complete Pass 1 before Pass 2” nonsense.
    Rae put it best in her forum post: “The tools aren't a conveyor belt. They're a workshop. You grab the tool you need, use it how you need it, put it back, grab another one. You're the craftsperson. These are just your chisels and sanders.”
